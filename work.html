During my undergraduate education, I was initially interested in exploring design as a means through which it's possible to facilitate adoption of new technologies. I operated under the assumption that while emerging technology can be misused, there are ways to use these technologies in a way that benefits society.

Academic Reading Assistance Tool (A-RAT)

BU: Forge Catalyst Designation Winner (Education, 2024)
view prototype
completed (view on Devpost)
problem space : exploring how AI can be used to help students learn to read academic literature

The Catalyst designathon is a 3-day design competition organized by Boston University's Forge organization in which teams of three have to create an interactive prototype for a conceptualized product, a write-up explaining the product's rationale and a 3-minute pitch video.

Many people struggle to understand academic writing. A-RAT (Academic Reading Assistance Tool) helps the user become more familiar with academic writing and improve their reading comprehension skills. By creating A-RAT, we also hope to make academic writing more accessible to people who might have an interest in reading about different topics found in academia, but aren’t used to reading the complicated language in most papers. A-RAT can also be a tool for high school and early college students making the transition towards reading more academic papers. This technology can especially be useful for students whose secondary education didn't teach them the skills to read academic papers.

My contribution to A-RAT was the problem space as well as designing and evaluating the concept for the site through applied research of reading comprehension education. Based on reading comprehension literature, I made informed design decisions that took shape into the overall vision of the website that I helped take from concept to wireframe, then a fully interactive prototype in Figma. In addition to making brand assets in Illustrator, I used a design system to create and refine various pages for the prototype. I also assisted in creating a 3-minute pitch video that was used in the judging process.

Facilitating Adoption of Autonomous Vehicles in Older Age Groups

senior capstone project
methods : interviews, literature review, prioritization activities
completed (read the full write-up)
research question : how might we best facilitate adoption of autonomous vehicles for older adults?

Autonomous vehicles (AVs) have made a lot of progress in terms of technology and public perception. This project covers an exploration of the most impactful ways to facilitate adoption of AVs in older age groups. Research done in 2021 led by Timo Gunthner in Germany showed that car companies are expanding their clientele beyond young drivers into the aging market. This research was followed up by Gunthner later that year in a study focused on determining AV needs specific for older adults and the importance of trust. While driving grows more difficult with age, some preliminary research has already been done regarding ways to make AVs more accessible for older population groups. This project uses applied knowledge sharing techniques to design solutions that facilitate adoption for older adults by exploring ways to help older adults build trust in AVs, design accessible solutions for older adults, and overall improve accessibility, public perception and perceived autonomy in the AV experience.

I also designed several activities to gauge perception of how older age groups feel about autonomous driving in general to understand where in the stages of autonomy a car would reach a “dealbreaker” point. While autonomous driving technology is still very early on in its development, I aimed to explore speculative design solutions that could be implemented as this technology matures to help older age groups feel more comfortable using it. Realizing this, I pivoted midway through my project to include adults aged 50-70 when I initially aimed for adults aged 70+. Adults in the 50-70 age group would likely advance into this age group by the time any design I created was ready for implementation, so I wanted their feedback as well in the creation of my final deliverables. In addition to these activities, I also conducted two literature reviews: one focused on autonomous driving and another focused on technology adoption in older age groups. I also conducted seven forty-minute interviews with adults over the age of seventy to understand their relationship with emerging technology and driving.

Through desk research of literacy education, conversations with older generations, I started to question if adoption was always a good end goal to work towards. Instead, I grew more concerned about if adoption of certain technologies would create new problems that outweigh possible benefit avenues. Thus began my interest in critiques of emerging technology and critical HCI.

Early Explorations of Ironies in AI-Assisted Design

research lab deliverable
methods : literature review, thematic coding
published as a Late-breaking Work at CHI 2025
research questions : what are UX designers’ perspectives on GenAI’s impact on UX practice? what potential ironies and concerns may arise as AI becomes integrated into the design process?

This paper studies early perceptions of how AI-assisted design would impact the design industry while juxtaposing those perceptions with Bainbridge’s Ironies of Automation to paint a picture of anticipated impacts of widespread adoption of AI-assisted design. My contribution to the paper was through gathering information (sourcing, reading), thematic coding in Dovetail, analysis exploring how automation literature might apply to AI-assisted design, drafting and revising the paper based on peer review prior to its publication. Specifically, I contributed most to the Background, Discussion and Limitations sections.

After a year of working in industry, I started work on my own non-funded study focused on exploring how confident people are that they can identify AI-generated news writing. I also aimed to find if there were any associated behaviors with a higher accuracy in AI-generated writing detection, such as more frequent reading or AI use.

Understanding False Confidence in AI Detection

independent study
methods : survey-based activity
drafting survey
research questions : does the presence and subsequent removal of an AI-assisted evaluative tool create misalignment between accuracy and confidence when identifying synthetic media?

While some literature has looked into humans’ accuracy when identifying synthetic media, a gap exists in measurements of that accuracy against a person’s confidence that their responses are accurate. I aim to explore through twenty five synthetic media identification tasks with self-reported assessments of confidence after each task. For analysis, participants’ confidence and accuracy scores will be compared to each other, as well as compared against their demographic data, generative AI use habits and reading habits.

The survey outline is as follows:

  • Demographics: age range, region, occupational status, education
  • GenAI Habits: if they use LLMs to write and in what contexts they do so
  • Reading Habits: how often and what types of material the participant read
  • Detection Tutorial: a couple of questions to check for attention and understanding of the task
  • Identification Activity: participants are presented with 25 2-3 images that the participant must label as human-made or AI-generated. Images will be generated from a variety of LLMs. Human-made images will be sourced from before 2020. Participants are asked their confidence in their response on a 5-point Likert scale after each labelling question.
  • Reflection: Participants are asked about their confidence overall in their responses and how easy/difficulty they thought the task was.

At this point, I am beginning the process of creating and gathering materials for this survey.
